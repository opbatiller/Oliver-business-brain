## SECTION 1: ORIGIN & FOUNDATION

### The Moment That Changed Everything

It all began with a verse. Colossians 3:23 — "Whatever you do, work at it with all your heart, as though you're working for the Lord and not for men." That was Oliver's guiding principle even while still at university.

During that time, his family was breaking apart quietly. His mother left for overseas work. His father — unemployed after the US departure from Clark Air Base in Pampanga — stayed home with the children, unable to find work, sitting with the weight of a life that had been taken from him.

Oliver's younger sister was forced to stop her Physical Therapy studies. Oliver was forced to work while studying. His university granted him a 50% scholarship — the Pinatubo Scholar — because of what he had achieved academically. In exchange, he served every weekend as a servant leader in outreach work at the Pinatubo Resettlement Center. The other 50% he earned working at Kentucky Fried Chicken.

There was one afternoon he came home and found his siblings — younger brother and sister — who had not eaten lunch, and now dinner was approaching. His father told him to go to the neighbor's store, list what they needed, and say they would pay later.

The store owner blocked his request.

She told him to go back to his father. Tell him to pay everything he already owed before she would give them another peso of credit.

Oliver was young. He stood there. And he cried.

He made a promise to himself that day — he would never go back to that community in poverty. He would not stay in a place where survival depended on the generosity of a neighbor who could say no.

He went to Manila. He applied to construction companies, engineering firms, corporations. Every time, the HR would look at his application and ask: "What is your university?" He watched applicants from Manila universities get hired while he was told, "We'll call you." He never got the call.

After many rejections, one company trusted him — an installation engineer role at ₱3,700 per month. He subtracted ₱1,200 for bed space. ₱100 for breakfast and lunch — combined, at 11 AM. No dinner. ₱500 for fare between Manila and Pampanga. He was breaking even on his own existence.

After three months, he resigned.

Walking past the DTI — the Department of Trade and Industry — something made him stop. On a quiet whim, almost as a joke, he walked in and registered a business name. He gave his apartment as the address. The name he chose: Ashekone Builders. His dream, written on a DTI form, was to do construction all over Asia.

Three days later, they approved it.

That was 1995. Oliver Batiller, 23 years old, with no capital and no connections, began knocking on construction fence doors — not as a job applicant, but as a subcontractor. He found Unicorn Construction Corporation. They gave him one small project: ₱60,000.

He delivered.

They gave him ₱200,000. Then ₱700,000. Then ₱3,000,000. Within one year, he had earned his first ₱3 million. He went back to his family's community — and left it. He bought property. He built the house his family lives in to this day.

From Ashekone Builders in 1995, he incorporated as AsiaCon Builders Inc. in 1997. Then Stone Global Philippines in 2006. ACBI Corp in 2007. And eventually, Global Eco Link Development Corporation — GELDC — his real estate, construction, and diversified business platform.

From Asia to Global. That is the story behind it.

---

### The People Who Shaped Him

**His Father**

His father was a perfectionist. Not university-educated, but meticulous. He did not want errors. He taught Oliver to be careful — not just in work, but in character. He passed on Colossians 3:23 as a moral foundation. He also taught from the Honda management playbook — the business administration style of the Japanese executives whose coats and trailers he had worked around. From his father, Oliver learned to be precise, principled, and patient.

**Gandhi Ngan**

In 2003, a British national based in Hong Kong and Macau named Gandhi Ngan — senior officer and client of Oliver's sister, who had hired her as an IT consultant — invited Oliver to become a partner and represent Stone Global in the Philippines.

Through Gandhi Ngan, Oliver learned importation, distribution, and how to position high-end building materials in the Philippine market. He studied imports from Europe, the US, the Middle East, and all across Asia. He developed a passion for innovative materials — eco-friendly, cutting-edge, forward-thinking.

It was through this partnership that Oliver's business motto was born:

*"Bringing innovations, eco-friendly and new technologies in building materials and constructions to the Philippines."*

That tagline now lives inside GELDC's DNA. From Global — eco-linked — to Philippine development.

---

### The Value Running His Decisions

Colossians 3:23. Always.

"Whatever you do, work at it with all your heart, as though working for the Lord and not for man."

Oliver does not approach problems as obstacles. He approaches them as situations that require proper guidance, proper direction, proper analysis, motivation, and inspiration. Every problem has a solution. The principle drives how he enters every project, every negotiation, every crisis.

## SECTION 2: CHARACTER UNDER PRESSURE

### The Hardest Chapter

Two moments defined his hardest periods.

The first: standing at the neighbor's store, being told no, walking back to his father with nothing. Being young, humiliated, and hungry — and choosing not to surrender.

The second: after building AsiaCon Builders from 1994 to 2001, watching everything he had created collapse due to cash flow mismanagement within the marriage. All the investment. All the revenue. Gone. Court cases followed. Legal pressure. Financial ruin.

He did not leave the field. He studied the root cause. He rebuilt the systems. By 2006, he had restarted — harder, faster, and with cleaner boundaries around every financial decision he made.

---

### The Risk That Defined Him

The most defining professional risk Oliver ever took was personal.

He removed his wife from all company signatories — every check, every account, every financial decision. It was not an operational calculation. It was a deeply personal line he had to draw across his own life to protect everything he had built.

From 2006 to 2025, that decision held. It controlled the cash outflow. It protected the companies. It cost him enormously in terms of the relationship. But it saved the business.

---

### The Mistake That Built Him

He trusted friends. He gave large amounts of money to people he believed in. Again and again, it disappeared.

The pattern repeated enough times to build something permanent in him — not bitterness, but discernment. He stopped being easily swayed by closeness. He became more careful, more calibrated, faster in his decisions, and harder to move on trust alone.

The losses were real. The lesson was worth more.

---

### Standing His Ground

The decision to remove his wife from the company signatories cost him in ways that went beyond business. It crossed the line between professional and personal. It brought emotional pressure, conflict, last-minute crises, and stress that accumulated over years.

He held the position anyway.

Because without it, the companies could not grow. Without it, decisions were compromised by emotion at the wrong moments. Without it, the people who depended on him — employees, clients, partners — would have been exposed to instability they didn't deserve.

He made the call and carried it.

---

## SECTION 3: THE WORK & THE WHY

### When He Knew

It was the moment the DTI approved the name Ashekone Builders.

What had started as a joke — a young man with no capital walking into a government office on a whim — became real. The business name was approved. The certificate was in his hands. He was not a job applicant anymore.

He was an entrepreneur.

Everything shifted from that moment.

---

### What He Does Differently

Oliver is proactive where others are reactive.

Most people in construction and development wait for problems to surface before addressing them. Oliver pre-plans. He sets clear objectives first, then builds the action plan around achieving them. He prepares for scenarios before they become crises — so that when pressure arrives, he is already positioned.

The mechanics: identify the objective, map the direction, build the plan before the situation demands it. Move first. Adjust in motion. Never wait for the last minute to know where you are.

---

### The Reframe That Changes Everything

*"Most people think poverty is a lack of money. I believe poverty is a lack of opportunity, systems, and execution. Most people, if they have no money, they cannot move. I believe with the proper direction, decision making, and action plan — money will follow."*

This is the belief that drove every company he built. He did not wait to be funded before starting. He built the direction first. The money came after.

---

### The Work That Made Him Proud

One housing and development initiative stands out — not for its size, but for what it represented.

The land existed. The vision existed. The demand for housing existed. But the project was fragmented — poor planning, weak coordination, no systems, no execution discipline. Oliver came in not as a builder but as a strategic partner. He brought direction, financial discipline, operational systems, and alignment between stakeholders.

Over time, the project moved from uncertainty to possibility. Decisions became aligned. The team gained confidence.

Behind every housing unit, Oliver knew, was a family hoping for stability. He had been one of those families — in a resettlement community after Mount Pinatubo, watching his parents struggle, watching his siblings go without lunch.

That is why housing development is not abstract for him. He built what he once desperately needed.

*"Development is not ultimately about concrete, steel, or buildings. It is about creating opportunities for people to build better futures."*

---

## SECTION 4: WORLDVIEW & DIRECTION

### What Everyone Gets Wrong

Financial management. Specifically: late reporting, no proper planning, and the weekend stress that comes from systems that were never built.

Oliver has lived this — the last-minute reports, the Sunday surprises, the pressure that arrives when it is already too late to change the outcome. In his view, the industry keeps repeating this mistake because most operators are too focused on the project in front of them to build the financial system behind it.

The result: cash flow crises that were entirely predictable — and entirely avoidable.

---

### How Success Changed Shape

When Oliver was young, success meant escaping poverty. Having stable income. A secure home. Financial independence. Never again being in the position of standing at a neighbor's store being told no.

As he entered business, success became growth — companies built, projects won, revenue generated, milestones reached.

Then something changed.

Every achievement created a new goal. Every milestone eventually became normal. The size of the victory kept escalating — but the feeling of it didn't stay.

What changed his perspective was watching what businesses and projects could do for other people's lives. He began to see that the most meaningful part of success was not what he accumulated, but what he helped create.

Today:

*"Success means creating opportunities for others. It means helping families find homes, helping employees build careers, helping entrepreneurs grow businesses, helping communities develop, and helping future generations benefit from the work we do today."*

The shift: from survival, to achievement, to contribution and legacy.

---

### Where This Is All Going

*"The future will not belong to the company with the most resources. It will belong to the organization that can best integrate people, technology, capital, and execution into one ecosystem."*

Oliver's read on the industry: the winners over the next decade will not be those with the most land or the most capital. They will be those who can integrate technology, AI, capital, partnerships, and execution into a single platform.

AI and technology will not replace builders, developers, or engineers. They will replace inefficiency. Organizations that continue relying on outdated systems and fragmented teams will be outpaced by those who embrace automation, data-driven decisions, and integrated execution.

The future of development is community-focused — not just structures, but environments where people can live, work, learn, grow businesses, and improve their quality of life.

Oliver's focus is no longer simply on construction or real estate. It is on building integrated platforms that connect people, resources, technology, infrastructure, and opportunity.

---

*"People may come to me for a project, but they leave with a bigger vision of what is possible for their future."*
